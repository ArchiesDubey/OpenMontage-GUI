# Witness Archive — finding episodes

## The test

An episode qualifies only if you can answer all five:

1. **What is the object?** One physical thing that still exists and can be photographed,
   held, or visited. Not a concept, not an event — a *thing*.
2. **Where is it now?** A museum case, a field, a sealed archive, a body. The answer is the
   closing beat.
3. **Who is named?** At least two real people with names. Anonymous victims are a research
   failure, not a fact of history.
4. **What did someone know, and when?** Every strong episode has a document, a memo, or a
   test result that proves the harm was foreseeable. This is the turn.
5. **Why does it still matter?** If the answer is only "it was bad", it's not an episode.

If the object is weak, the episode is weak — no amount of narration rescues it.

## The four territories

Roam freely across all four. The device unifies them; the topic does not need to.

| Territory | Shape | Monetisation |
|---|---|---|
| **Industrial & engineering disasters** | a decision on paper killed people | safest; lowest competition |
| **Dark medicine & experimentation** | curiosity outrunning consent | highest CTR, highest ad risk |
| **Origins of dangerous things** | a substance or practice traced to its first use | safe, evergreen, most crowded |
| **Institutional cover-ups** | what was legal at the time | safe; strongest "turn" beats |

## Evergreen check

The channel's premise is videos that still earn in three years. Before committing:

- Does the topic depend on a news cycle? Reject.
- Would the episode need re-cutting if something new emerged? Prefer settled history.
- Is there existing search demand, or are you building an audience from zero? Both are
  viable — but know which you're doing. Low-competition verticals (aviation, industrial,
  maritime disasters) trade reach for durability.

## Anti-saturation

Broad "dark history" is heavily saturated; narration-over-maps-and-timelines is the default
format in that lane. You do **not** win on facts — every one of these stories is on
Wikipedia. You win on:

1. the object-first cold open (most channels open with context),
2. the two-register visual system (most channels have one look),
3. named people and the specific document (most channels stay abstract),
4. restraint (most channels sensationalise).

If a proposed episode's only differentiator is "we cover it too", drop it.

## Sourcing candidates

Good hunting grounds: industrial accident inquiry reports, museum accession records, court
discovery documents, occupational-disease literature, decommissioned facility inventories,
and the footnotes of better-known stories — the object is usually mentioned once, in
passing, by someone who didn't realise it was the story.

## Recording them

Every candidate goes into `.claude/skills/witness-archive/IDEAS.md` with: the object, the
territory, the named people, the "who knew" turn, monetisation risk, and a one-line note on
why it earns in three years. Never keep candidates only in conversation.
