# Witness Archive — narration voice

Derived from transcript analysis of the reference channels (Ink Explainer 200 wpm,
Chat History 155–184 wpm, Historically 164–171 wpm) plus the channel's own device.

## The hook formula

**Open on one concrete, verifiable, physical thing. Then pull back to the pattern.**

This is the single most reusable device in the entire reference set. Ink Explainer's
strongest video opens on archaeologists cracking a 2,700-year-old tomb in western China —
a specific man, blue-eyed, buried with a harp and two pounds of still-green cannabis — and
only *then* widens into a 12,000-year story.

The inverse also works: open on the viewer's present moment ("Right now, you're probably
drunk"), then drop into deep time. Use it sparingly — the object-first version is stronger
for this channel because it is what the whole format promises.

**Never open with:**
- a definition ("Radium is a chemical element...")
- a throat-clear ("In this video we're going to look at...")
- a rhetorical question ("Have you ever wondered...")
- the channel's own branding

The first sentence should be a fact so specific it sounds invented.

## Voice

- **Low, unhurried, measured.** The horror carries itself; the narrator does not push it.
- **Never sensational.** No "you won't believe", no "shocking truth", no breathless
  escalation. Understatement is the register. If a fact is horrifying, state it flatly and
  move on — the flatness is what lands.
- **Second person sparingly.** Reserve "you" for moments of genuine implication.
- **Present tense for scenes, past tense for consequence.**
- **Name people.** Not "workers" — *Grace Fryer, Katherine Schaub, Quinta McDonald*. The
  specificity is the entire moral engine of the channel.

## Beat discipline

**One sentence = one visual beat.** Write so that each sentence can be a single image.
This makes the whole downstream pipeline (script → narration → transcribe → image prompts
→ render) line up cleanly without re-cutting.

- Vary sentence length hard. A long, winding, clause-heavy sentence that builds. Then four
  words.
- No sentence should need two images to illustrate.
- A paragraph is roughly a scene.

## Structure (8–20 min)

1. **Cold open** (0:00–0:45) — the object. No context, no framing. Just the thing.
2. **The widening** (0:45–2:00) — what the object implies. State the question the episode
   will answer.
3. **Body** — chronological or causal, 3–5 movements. Cut to the **chalk register** for
   any mechanism, map, timeline, or chain of command.
4. **The turn** — the moment the harm becomes undeniable. Usually a document, a number, or
   a person who knew.
5. **The residue** (final 60–90s) — what survives today. Return to the opening object.
   Close on the physical present, not a moral.

Do not editorialise in the closing line. The strongest ending is a fact.

## Things that are banned

- Gore, injury description for its own sake, or dwelling on suffering. The horror is in the
  **specificity of the record**, not the wound. This is also what keeps the channel
  monetisable.
- Speculation stated as fact. If a claim is contested, say it is contested.
- "Historians believe" as a hedge for something you didn't verify.
- Any sentence that could open any other history video.

## Fact discipline

Every factual claim in the script needs a source in the `research_brief`. Dark history is
exactly the genre where sloppy claims metastasise — and a channel whose entire promise is
"this really happened" cannot afford one debunked episode. Where sources conflict, say so
in the narration; the honesty is a feature.
